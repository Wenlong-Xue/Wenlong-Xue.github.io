MOF glasses

MOF glasses & hybrid glass frameworks

Coordination chemistry across crystalline and amorphous states.

What are MOF glasses?

MOF glasses are amorphous or glassy materials derived from metal-organic frameworks, a class of crystalline coordination networks built from metal nodes and organic linkers. In a crystalline MOF, the long-range periodic arrangement of these building blocks is central to structure determination and pore design. In a MOF glass, that long-range order is disrupted, but important elements of local metal-ligand coordination can remain. This makes metal-organic framework glasses different from simple decomposed solids: they are disordered materials with chemical memory of the framework from which they came.

The scientific opportunity is that MOF glasses and hybrid glass frameworks can combine molecular tunability with processability. Many crystalline MOFs are difficult to shape into dense films, membranes, or device-compatible layers without binders or grain boundaries. A glassy framework, by contrast, may be processed through melting, flux-assisted transformation, mechanochemical synthesis, or direct amorphisation. The resulting material can retain useful framework chemistry while becoming easier to integrate into practical architectures.

Metal-organic framework glasses and local structure

The phrase metal-organic framework glasses emphasises that glass formation is not merely a loss of crystallinity. Local structure matters. Metal coordination geometry, linker flexibility, defect chemistry, and charge-balancing ions can determine whether a framework melts, amorphises, decomposes, or forms a stable glass. Understanding this local structure requires methods that do not rely only on Bragg diffraction. Synchrotron pair distribution function analysis, X-ray absorption spectroscopy, solid-state NMR, vibrational spectroscopy, microscopy, and modelling all help describe how coordination environments survive or reorganise in the glassy state.

For hybrid glass frameworks, this local order can be used to tune free volume, chemical accessibility, mechanical behaviour, and transport. A productive design strategy is to treat disorder as a controllable feature rather than a failure of crystallinity. This is why research on MOF glasses often moves between synthesis, structure, and processing rather than treating them as separate problems.

Amorphous proton conductors and proton conduction

Amorphous proton conductors are a key target for framework-derived glass research. Proton conduction depends on carrier concentration, hydrogen-bonding environments, acid-base interactions, humidity or water activity, and the dynamics of local structural motifs. Hybrid frameworks can provide chemically defined sites for proton carriers while also allowing the connectivity and softness of an amorphous matrix. This creates opportunities for proton conduction in materials that are more robust or more processable than conventional molecular salts, polymers, or crystalline powders.

In Wenlong Xue’s research, proton conduction is linked to the broader question of how local coordination order supports transport in disordered materials. Impedance spectroscopy provides macroscopic conductivity, while NMR and local-structure probes help identify how protons move and how the host network responds. The goal is to develop amorphous proton conductors that can be connected to energy and separation technologies rather than studied only as isolated powders.

Mechanochemical synthesis and glass formation

Mechanochemical synthesis offers a powerful route to MOF glasses because mechanical energy can drive transformations that are difficult to access thermally. Ball milling can introduce defects, reduce particle size, activate interfaces, promote ligand exchange, or induce amorphisation. In some systems, mechanochemical synthesis enables melting, glass formation, and glass-ceramic conversion in frameworks that would otherwise decompose or remain crystalline. This makes mechanochemistry a useful tool for exploring the energy landscape between crystal, amorphous solid, liquid, and glass.

Mechanochemical routes are also attractive for sustainability and scale-up because they can reduce solvent use and open pathways to metastable phases. For MOF glasses, the most important questions are how mechanical activation changes local coordination, whether the amorphous product is a true glass or a defect-rich solid, and how the resulting material can be processed further.

Thin films, membranes and device integration

The value of MOF glasses increases when they can be shaped into useful forms. Thin films and membranes are therefore central to translating hybrid glass frameworks into devices. Continuous glassy layers may reduce grain-boundary problems found in crystalline films, while flux melting or meltable framework chemistry may enable coating, patterning, or interfacial bonding. Such processing routes are relevant to nanofiltration, selective separations, proton-conducting layers, catalytic interfaces, fuel cells, and electrolysers.

Device integration requires materials that can survive thermal cycling, humidity changes, mechanical handling, and contact with electrodes or membranes. MOF glasses offer an unusual combination of inorganic-like connectivity and organic-linker tunability. This combination is why they are promising for hybrid energy and separation systems.
