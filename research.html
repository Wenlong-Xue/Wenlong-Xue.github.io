MOF Glasses, Proton Conductors, Processing and Devices

Wenlong Xue’s research sits at the boundary between crystalline coordination chemistry and glass science. Metal-organic frameworks are usually discussed as ordered porous crystals, yet many framework solids can also be driven into liquid, amorphous, or glassy states while retaining elements of local metal-ligand connectivity. MOF glasses and broader metal-organic framework glasses therefore offer a route to materials that combine coordination-network chemistry with the shaping, flow, and integration advantages normally associated with glasses. The central question is how local structure survives disorder, and how this local structure controls transport, mechanical stability, porosity, and compatibility with practical processing routes.

Hybrid glass frameworks are especially attractive because they allow molecular-level chemistry to be connected with macroscale form. In crystalline frameworks, long-range order can make structure determination elegant, but it can also limit how the material is processed into films, membranes, or device-ready architectures. In amorphous frameworks and MOF glasses, the loss of periodicity is not simply a defect. It can create new free-volume distributions, interfacial environments, and ion-transport pathways. The research therefore focuses on how framework composition, ligand chemistry, cation modification, and synthesis history determine the balance between retained local order and useful disorder.

Amorphous Proton Conductors

A major theme is the design of amorphous proton conductors based on hybrid and framework-derived materials. Proton conduction depends on the density, connectivity, and dynamics of proton carriers, but also on the local structure that supports hopping or vehicular transport. Framework glasses can provide immobilised coordination sites, tunable organic linkers, and chemically defined environments that are difficult to reproduce in conventional inorganic glasses or polymer electrolytes. By studying amorphous proton conductors through impedance spectroscopy, solid-state NMR, diffraction, and local-structure methods, the work aims to identify how disorder can be used deliberately rather than tolerated as a limitation.

Mechanochemical Synthesis

Mechanochemical synthesis provides another route to glass formation and amorphisation. Instead of relying only on melt-quenching, ball milling and related mechanical activation methods can transform coordination frameworks under conditions where thermal melting is inaccessible or chemically undesirable. Mechanochemistry can introduce defects, promote ligand rearrangement, modify particle interfaces, and in some cases trigger genuine amorphous or glassy states. This approach is important for MOF glasses because many frameworks decompose before melting, while others require fluxes, ion modification, or pressure-assisted pathways. Mechanochemical synthesis gives access to metastable materials and glass-ceramic conversions that can reveal how a framework collapses, reorganises, and partially preserves coordination motifs.

Thin Films and Membranes

Processing is a recurring challenge in framework chemistry. A powder can be structurally beautiful but still difficult to incorporate into a device. Thin films, membranes, and patterned layers are therefore central to the research direction. MOF glasses and meltable or flux-processable frameworks can potentially be cast, laminated, or converted into continuous layers with fewer grain-boundary problems than crystalline powders. This matters for nanofiltration, selective transport, proton exchange layers, and catalytic interfaces. The research connects synthesis with film formation, surface compatibility, thickness control, and post-processing so that glassy framework materials can be evaluated in geometries closer to real technologies.

Device Integration

Device integration requires more than high conductivity or porosity in an isolated sample. Materials must tolerate interfaces, temperature windows, humidity changes, mechanical handling, and contact with electrodes, membranes, or catalytic layers. Hybrid glass frameworks may help bridge this gap because they retain chemical tunability while becoming easier to process into coherent architectures. For energy technologies, the long-term goal is to connect amorphous proton conductors and MOF-derived glasses with medium-temperature fuel cells, electrolysers, and separation devices. For each application, structure-property relationships must be linked to manufacturable form.

Structure-Property Feedback

A practical research cycle begins with a framework composition or processing concept, then follows the material through amorphisation, glass formation, local-structure analysis, transport testing, and shape control. This feedback loop is important because a high-performance framework glass rarely emerges from a single parameter. Ion identity, linker chemistry, residual porosity, water uptake, thermal history, and defect density can all influence proton conduction or ion transport. By comparing crystalline precursors, mechanically treated solids, melt-quenched glasses, flux-processed materials, and thin films, it becomes possible to distinguish intrinsic transport from artefacts caused by interfaces, particle packing, or sample history. This is especially relevant for amorphous proton conductors, where conductivity can depend strongly on hydration, acid-base chemistry, and percolation through local environments.

The broader research strategy is therefore to move between synthesis, local-structure characterisation, transport measurements, and processing. Synchrotron PDF, XAS, solid-state NMR, impedance spectroscopy, microscopy, and modelling each provide a different view of how coordination networks behave beyond crystallinity. Together they help answer why some frameworks form stable glasses, why some amorphous networks conduct protons or ions more effectively, and how chemical modification can tune the transition from crystal to liquid to glass.
